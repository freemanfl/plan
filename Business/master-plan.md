## Core System Foundation

### Funnel Logic Comes First

Every website begins with the sequence of buyer questions and persuasion logic before copy, layout, visual design, animation, or technical implementation.

The initial reference models are **Oak Harbor Web Designs** and **ShipFast**.

Their shared funnel logic is approximately:

**Promise → Problem → Solution / Mechanism → Benefits → Trust / Authority → Exact Offer → Objection / Risk Reduction → Proof → Price / Value Justification → Social Proof → CTA**

Oak Harbor is the primary reference for **trust, service, responsibility, recurring support, and small-business positioning**.

ShipFast is the primary reference for **pain, outcome, simplicity, quantified value, and strong persuasion**.

The studio's production system should use this logic as the structural backbone, then adapt it by business type. Layout, design, SEO, animations, and polish are later layers built on top of the funnel—not substitutes for it.

# 1. Client Funnel

The first part of the business system is acquiring clients and understanding how a small-business owner moves from having a need to deciding to buy a website.

## 1.1 Client Acquisition

The essential first problem is finding clients.

Rather than starting with generic advertising channels, the goal is to understand **where people who are likely to buy a small-business website naturally appear, what events create that need, and what administrative or commercial processes they already have to go through**.

Questions to research include:

- Who typically buys a first website or replaces a weak existing website?
- What events trigger the purchase?
- Where do newly created or changing small businesses interact with institutions, services, suppliers, communities, or government offices?
- Are there legal or administrative steps that create natural marketing opportunities?
- Which physical and digital locations concentrate people who are starting or formalizing businesses?
- Are there equivalents to highly targeted advertising around places such as immigration offices, where the audience has an unusually predictable need?

The objective is to map the likely client journey and identify places where website demand naturally appears instead of relying only on broad advertising.

## 1.2 Buying Decision

Once a potential client is aware that they may need a website, the next stage is understanding the buying decision:

- What exactly are we selling?
- What does it cost?
- Why should the client choose this offer rather than another studio, freelancer, DIY builder, or no website at all?
- What expectations need to be set before purchase?

# 2. The Offer

## 2.1 Core Product

The core product is a **good-looking, modern, fast small-business website or landing page**.

It is intentionally not positioned as a highly bespoke agency project. The product should be:

- functional;
- visually above average;
- fast;
- technically solid;
- suitable as a reliable home for a small business online;
- easy to maintain and expand later.

The intention is to build a repeatable production system that allows a surprisingly good result at a relatively low price.

## 2.2 SEO

The offer includes **good on-page SEO and basic local SEO foundations**.

This should improve the site's structure and its ability to be understood and indexed by search engines, but it should not be sold as an instant path to high rankings or guaranteed traffic.

### Current limitation: Off-page SEO

Off-page SEO is **not currently part of the offer** because the capability and process have not yet been developed sufficiently.

It is an important capability to research and potentially add later.

## 2.3 Design Positioning

The website should look better than the typical low-cost small-business website.

The aim is not to sell a completely custom, agency-level artistic production. Instead, the system should produce:

- strong fundamentals;
- clean contemporary design;
- good typography and spacing;
- good performance;
- polished visual details;
- tasteful animation;
- occasional higher-end design touches inspired by excellent modern websites.

The result should feel elevated without pretending that every project is a bespoke award-site engagement.

## 2.4 Included Services and Possible Bonuses

The basic package should cover the practical website lifecycle rather than only delivering files.

Potential included services or bonuses include:

- design;
- website construction;
- hosting;
- on-page SEO;
- local SEO setup;
- Google Business / Maps-related setup or assistance;
- simple booking functionality where appropriate;
- tasteful animations and interactive details;
- basic ongoing maintenance.

The exact boundaries and economics of these extras still need to be defined.

## 2.5 Maintenance

Maintenance is an important part of the value proposition.

The client should not receive unlimited redesign work or endless stylistic experimentation, but ordinary business-driven changes should be easy.

Examples include:

- replacing images;
- updating business information;
- changing prices or services;
- updating opening hours;
- adding or replacing content;
- other reasonable operational changes.

The goal is to make the website something the client does not need to worry about.

## 2.6 Ownership and Exit

Clients should be allowed to take the website away if they want.

The business should not depend on trapping clients technically or contractually.

A client could potentially subscribe for only a few months, leave with a website that cost much less than a traditional equivalent, and still feel that they received an unusually good deal.

This is acceptable because the long-term business model should be attractive because of ongoing convenience and peace of mind rather than lock-in.

## 2.7 What Is Really Being Sold

The physical product is a website.

The deeper product is **peace of mind**:

- the website exists;
- it looks professional;
- it works;
- it remains maintained;
- changes can be requested without technical knowledge;
- the business owner does not need to manage hosting, code, plugins, updates, or routine web work.

The strategy is to provide substantial value early through a strong initial website and make the ongoing relationship worthwhile through maintenance, support, and convenience.

## 2.8 Ideal Client and Expectation Setting

The offer is best suited to a practical small-business owner who understands the scope.

The client is not buying unlimited custom design or a guaranteed marketing machine. They are buying a solid, attractive, functional online presence at a favorable price.

A website can capture opportunities that might otherwise disappear—for example, someone researching the business after hours who would leave because there is no credible website or insufficient information.

SEO can increase discoverability, especially locally, but the site should not be presented as an automatic source of endless customers.

# 3. Production System

The business depends on building a repeatable system capable of producing high-quality websites efficiently.

AI will be an important part of this, but the larger challenge is **architecting the pipeline around the AI** so that it receives enough structured information, constraints, examples, and reusable components to behave predictably.

## 3.1 Variable Client Preparation

Clients will arrive with very different levels of preparation.

Possible inputs include:

- a complete existing website;
- an incomplete or outdated website;
- business descriptions;
- existing copy;
- logos;
- photographs;
- scattered images;
- social-media content;
- service lists;
- very little usable material.

The system needs to accept these differences rather than requiring every client to prepare a perfect content package.

## 3.2 Client Intake

A structured Google Form is the initial candidate for client intake.

Its purpose is to gather the information required by the production system in a predictable format while remaining easy for the client.

The intake should eventually collect both explicit answers and references to existing material.

## 3.3 Input Analysis

All supplied client material should be analyzed, sorted, and adapted into the studio's existing system.

The pipeline should be capable of:

- gathering supplied images;
- inspecting existing websites;
- understanding what information already exists;
- identifying useful content;
- establishing hierarchy;
- deciding which information belongs in which section;
- mapping the client's material into the appropriate funnel/template;
- deciding what is missing.

Images should also be evaluated for their likely role in the website rather than simply placed wherever they happen to fit.

## 3.4 Copy

The system should support both:

- using and improving client-provided copy;
- generating new copy where necessary.

The production process should determine which approach is appropriate based on the quality and completeness of the supplied material.

# 4. Website Architecture System

The architecture layer translates business purpose and funnel logic into a controlled page structure before visual styling is applied.

## 4.1 Site Purpose Comes Before Layout

Before selecting a funnel or layout, determine what the website is primarily trying to do. Possible modes include:

- sales / conversion;
- showcase / portfolio;
- information;
- booking / reservation;
- trust / credibility;
- a hybrid of several modes.

A hard sales funnel should not be forced onto every website. A coach or barber may be conversion-heavy, an artist may be showcase-first, and a restaurant may combine trust, information, menu/location, and booking.

This should be known early in client intake because it changes downstream funnel and section choices.

Current structural order:

**Site Purpose → Funnel → Content → Section Structure → Layout → Design → Theme → Effects / Enrichment → Final Polish / QA**

## 4.2 Funnel Structures

The system should use a limited number of predefined funnel structures. The core persuasion logic may be consistent, but different business categories may require different sequences.

Examples:

- a gym or coach may use a stronger sales/conversion sequence;
- an art gallery may be showcase-first;
- a restaurant may mix credibility, information, menu/location, and booking.

The objective is **one production system + a small library of proven funnel structures**, rather than inventing the information architecture from scratch for every client.

## 4.3 Layout as Structured Data

Layouts should not be stored only as screenshots or vague labels such as “modern hero.” They should be represented as structured blueprints that AI can select and populate predictably.

For each section layout, store as much of the following as useful:

- section type and layout ID;
- optional family/category;
- grid or normalized geometry;
- primary regions and relative widths;
- vertical alignment / anchor points;
- content stack and element order;
- required and optional content;
- media count, shape, and aspect requirements;
- sensible copy limits;
- CTA count and placement;
- proof / trust placement;
- relative visual weight of text, media, and proof;
- whitespace behavior;
- desktop/tablet/mobile collapse rules;
- when the layout works well and when to avoid it;
- reference screenshot / source site;
- reusable implementation component where available.

The chosen layout should take into account the selected business/funnel type, intake answers, client media, existing/generated copy, and the amount and quality of available content.

Each section should have several compatible layout variants so sites do not all look identical while still remaining inside a controlled system.

The long-term goal is to reduce visual design from an open-ended wildcard into a constrained composition problem. AI should receive a layout blueprint rather than an instruction such as “make it look like Stripe.”

Example abstraction:

```text
hero_split_03
12-column grid
text: cols 1-5
visual: cols 7-12
text vertically centered
headline + body + trust row + 2 CTAs
large proof-carrying visual on right
mobile: text first, visual second
```

## 4.4 Layout Is Structural; Composition Rules Belong to Design

The layout layer answers **what block is being used and how its internal elements are arranged**.

Rules about how multiple sections behave together belong to the later design stage. Examples include page rhythm, avoiding two visually heavy sections back-to-back, balancing text and media, spacing transitions, color continuity, and animation intensity.

This distinction should remain explicit so structural layout selection and whole-page visual composition do not become mixed into one uncontrolled step.

## 4.5 Hero Layout Reference Library

Hero sections are the first concrete section type being modeled in detail.

Current reference families / examples include:

- **Linear** — restrained product showcase;
- **Raycast** — expressive product showcase;
- **Stripe** — strong proposition combined with a rich visual system;
- **Oak Harbor** — service-sales / trust-first split hero;
- **ShipFast** — centered direct-response / offer-first hero.

The purpose is not to copy branding, but to capture reusable geometry, content order, visual mass, and selling behavior.

Oak Harbor can be abstracted roughly as:

**eyebrow/category → headline → differentiation/price → CTA pair → client-work visual**

ShipFast can be abstracted roughly as:

**outcome headline → mechanism sentence → CTA → scarcity/proof row → product/proof visual**

The reference library should become increasingly granular: each strong real-world section should be translated into a structured layout spec rather than left only as an inspiration link.

## 4.6 Small-Business Reference Baseline: Fraser Water Services

Fraser Water Services is a useful example of a clean, professional, reproducible small-business site. Its quality comes largely from disciplined composition rather than unusual technology.

Useful pattern:

**Hero → trust / proof → service focus → service grid → testimonial → CTA → credentials / footer**

The value of this reference is that a controlled system should be able to reproduce most of this level of quality through strong spacing, typography, image placement, and consistent component rules.

## 4.7 Small-Business Reference Baseline: IHI

IHI is another strong example of a visually polished but structurally ordinary small-business landing page.

Its facade can be reduced approximately to:

**Hero → Services → Trust / Statistics → Testimonials → Final Conversion Block → Footer**

The important lesson is that professional appearance comes primarily from the exact composition of ordinary blocks rather than exotic page architecture.

Useful reusable primitives visible in this type of site include:

- a split hero with proof integrated into the visual side;
- a three-card services grid with consistent media ratios and internal spacing;
- a four-column oversized-stat trust band;
- testimonial layouts that adapt to variable quote length;
- a final CTA that compresses and repeats the main promise after the page has established trust.

These should be captured as reusable layout primitives, not merely copied as finished pages.

# 5. Visual Design System

The visual layer sits above the structural layout. It is the main wildcard perceived by the client and therefore deserves unusually strong systemization.

## 5.1 The Variable Layer Is Visual Design

Many technical fundamentals can eventually become deterministic checklists. The harder variable is how ordinary content is made to look intentionally composed and professionally designed.

The design system should therefore focus heavily on controlling:

- hero and section geometry;
- image placement;
- whitespace;
- typography scale;
- card dimensions;
- overlaps and layering;
- alignment;
- section transitions;
- backgrounds and decorative surfaces;
- motion and micro-interaction.

A key objective is to turn these from subjective improvisation into reusable data, rules, and reference libraries.

## 5.2 Base Visual Design

The system should determine elements such as:

- colors;
- typography;
- spacing;
- imagery treatment;
- borders and radii;
- backgrounds and surfaces;
- component styling;
- visual hierarchy.

The objective is to generate a coherent visual identity without requiring a fully custom design process every time.

## 5.3 Whole-Page Composition Rules

The design system should contain compatibility / composition rules that govern how selected layouts work together across the page.

Examples:

- avoid repetitive section compositions;
- avoid consecutive visually heavy sections where it hurts rhythm;
- control alternation between text-heavy and media-heavy sections;
- keep spacing transitions intentional;
- maintain color and surface continuity;
- balance visual weight across the page;
- control animation intensity so the site does not become noisy.

These rules belong after layout selection, not inside individual layout definitions.

## 5.4 Theme

A **theme** is a loose semantic motif derived from the business, subject, place, history, culture, product, or personality. It is not a rigid visual template. It gives the design system contextually appropriate ideas.

Examples:

- history museum → archaeology/history motifs, archival treatments, timeline ideas, subtle arrowhead cursor;
- football → pitch geometry, ball-path motion, scoreboard-inspired details;
- dance → movement, flowing transitions, choreography-inspired gallery behavior.

Theme should influence optional details, motion, decoration, imagery treatment, and interactions where appropriate.

**Theme should enrich the website, not turn it into a gimmick.**

For AI, theme should act as a creative constraint: identify a small number of tasteful places where the subject can influence design without damaging usability.

## 5.5 Design Intelligence / Effects Library

Build a curated library of strong effects and interaction ideas found on high-quality websites instead of expecting AI to reinvent them on every project.

Each effect should ideally store:

- name and category;
- screenshot, video, or source reference;
- plain-English description of what happens;
- trigger (scroll, hover, cursor, load, etc.);
- target element;
- initial and final state;
- timing / easing / stagger where known;
- why the effect works;
- appropriate section and business types;
- required media/content;
- intensity: subtle / noticeable / centerpiece;
- mobile fallback;
- performance and accessibility considerations;
- implementation/component/code reference where appropriate;
- concise instructions that explain the mechanism to AI.

The distinction is:

**Layout Library = structural arrangement of information.**

**Effects Library = optional visual / interaction enhancement applied later.**

The library may include very simple effects. Small viewport reveals, card lifts, image scale on hover, subtle clipping/masking, and staggered entrances can materially improve perceived quality without requiring complex bespoke engineering.

## 5.6 Signature / High-End Effect

On top of the solid base design, websites should receive at least one more memorable contemporary design element where appropriate.

This may be adapted from techniques observed on excellent modern or award-winning websites: unusual gallery interaction, sophisticated lighting, distinctive transition, attractive scroll behavior, or an advanced treatment of an otherwise ordinary section.

It should not dominate the website. Its purpose is to add a controlled layer of perceived craftsmanship — the studio's small **“Michelin-star touch.”**

## 5.7 Design Grammar from IHI-Type Sites

The polished appearance of sites such as IHI can be explained by a small number of repeatable design levers:

- generous section spacing;
- strong but limited typography ratios;
- restricted readable content widths;
- a small number of dominant visual masses per section;
- consistent radii, spacing, button shapes, and card padding;
- repetition with controlled variation;
- subtle motion rather than constant animation.

These are candidates for future design tokens and rules.

A useful mental model is to keep three layers separate:

```text
LAYOUT BLUEPRINTS
geometry / regions / element order / responsive behavior

DESIGN TOKENS & COMPOSITION
spacing / typography / widths / radii / surfaces / visual rhythm

MOTION & EFFECT TOKENS
reveal / stagger / hover / masking / image motion / themed effects
```

This separation is central to making visually strong sites reproducible by the system.

## 5.8 Required Preparation

This approach depends on significant preparation before it becomes efficient.

The studio will need reusable libraries of:

- layouts;
- components;
- funnel structures;
- section variants;
- visual styles;
- effects;
- interaction patterns;
- rules describing when each option should and should not be used.

The quality of the AI output will depend heavily on the quality of this underlying design system.

# 6. Technical Website System

Technical quality is important to the eventual offer, but it is intentionally separated from the visual design system because much of it can become a deterministic checklist and reusable implementation layer.

The technical system should eventually cover:

## 6.1 Responsive and Structural Fundamentals

- responsive behavior across desktop, tablet, and mobile;
- navigation and footer fundamentals;
- semantic structure;
- accessibility basics;
- technical consistency across reusable components.

## 6.2 SEO and Search Foundations

- on-page SEO;
- basic local SEO;
- heading structure;
- metadata;
- sitemap;
- robots directives;
- schema / structured data where useful;

**Off-page SEO is a future capability and is not currently part of the core promise.**

## 6.3 Performance

- page speed;

- contact forms;

- booking/reservation integrations;

## 6.5 Domain, DNS, Email, Hosting and Deployment

- domain setup and transfer basics;
- DNS;
- business email setup/troubleshooting basics;

- deployment;

## 6.6 Analytics and Conversion Tracking

- basic traffic analytics;

## 6.7 Legal / Privacy Basics

AI should perform as much routine checking as practical, with human responsibility remaining at the final approval stage.

This section is intentionally less developed for now. The present production-system work prioritizes the variable visual layer because it is the part most visible to clients and the hardest to make predictable.

# 7. Post-Launch Client Service

After launch, clients need an extremely easy way to request changes.

The client-facing process should feel much simpler than the technical system behind it.

Possible channels include:

- email;
- WhatsApp or similar messaging;
- another deliberately simple request interface.

Clients should not need to understand tickets, Git repositories, code, deployment systems, or technical terminology.

## 7.1 AI-Assisted Change Pipeline

The long-term goal is a highly automated maintenance pipeline.

A possible flow:

1. Client sends a normal-language request.
2. AI reads and classifies the request.
3. AI identifies the relevant website and codebase.
4. AI determines the likely required changes.
5. AI prepares the code/content change.
6. A human reviews the proposed work.
7. The approved change is deployed.

The ambition is that a human—potentially the user's wife in an operational role—mainly reviews and approves AI-prepared work instead of manually performing every change.

The exact safe level of automation still needs to be determined.

## 7.2 Automation vs Human Work

A major system-design task will be deciding:

- which steps AI can perform autonomously;
- which require human review;
- what information AI must have before attempting a task;
- what safeguards are required;
- which tasks are economical to automate;
- which tasks are faster or safer to handle manually.

The objective is to minimize operating cost and labor while maintaining reliable quality.

# 8. Business Economics

The production and maintenance architecture needs to be designed around strong unit economics.

The underlying assumption is that a well-prepared reusable system can make the initial website much cheaper to produce than a traditional custom workflow.

This allows the studio to provide high initial value without requiring a large upfront payment.

Long-term profitability should come from the combination of:

- efficient production;
- reusable infrastructure;
- automation;
- hosting;
- maintenance;
- continuing client relationships;
- low marginal cost per website.

The exact pricing model and cost structure remain to be designed.
